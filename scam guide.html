📖 Scam Guide

Plain-English advice on spotting scams and protecting yourself. Written for the UK.

Stop. Think. Verify.Real organisations will never mind you taking time to check. Anyone who rushes you, or says “don’t tell anyone”, is a warning sign. If something feels wrong, hang up, close the message, and contact the organisation yourself using a number or website you already trust.

Common warning signs

  • Out of the blue: you didn’t ask for the call, text, or message.
  • Urgency or fear: “act now”, “your account is compromised”, “you’ll be arrested”.
  • Unusual payment: gift cards, crypto, bank transfers to a “safe account”.
  • Secrecy: told not to speak to your bank, family, or the police.
  • Too good to be true: guaranteed returns, surprise prizes, easy money.
  • Asks for codes or passwords: including one-time passcodes sent to your phone.
  • Impersonation: pretends to be your bank, the police, HMRC, a delivery firm, or someone you know. Caller ID and logos can be faked.

Common scams (tap to open)

Bank or police “safe account” calls

Someone claiming to be your bank, fraud team, or the police says your money is at risk and you must move it to a “safe account”. They may know some of your details and can make the call appear to come from a genuine number. No bank or police officer will ever ask you to do this. Hang up, wait a few minutes, and call 159 (it connects you to your bank) or use the number on your card.

Fake delivery and parcel texts

A text says you missed a delivery and must pay a small fee or “reschedule” via a link. The link steals your card details. Don’t click. Check with the courier through their official app or website, or look at your order account directly.

HMRC, tax refund and government scams

Messages or calls offering a tax rebate or threatening arrest or penalties. HMRC will never send a text offering a refund or asking for payment or personal details. Don’t reply or click. Check GOV.UK directly if you’re worried.

“Hi Mum” and family emergency messages

A text or WhatsApp from an unknown number says “Hi Mum, I’ve lost my phone” and soon asks for urgent money. Call the person on the number you already have, or ask a question only they could answer. Agreeing a family safe word in advance helps.

Romance scams

Someone you’ve met online builds a close bond, but can never meet or video call, and then has a crisis that needs money. Never send money, gift cards, or crypto to someone you haven’t met in person, however real the relationship feels. Scammers are patient and skilled, and it is not your fault if this happens to you.

Investment and crypto scams

Promises of high or guaranteed returns, often promoted on social media, by celebrity “endorsements”, or by someone you’ve befriended online. Check that a firm is authorised on the Financial Conduct Authority (FCA) register and look at the FCA Warning List before you invest. Be very wary of anyone who contacts you unprompted about investing.

Tech support and remote access

A pop-up or call says your computer is infected, or your broadband or bank needs “fixing”, and asks you to install a tool that lets them control your device. Never give remote access to someone who contacted you. Genuine firms don’t cold-call about viruses.

Fake jobs and “task” scams

A message offers easy work from home, such as liking videos or boosting products, with good pay. Later you’re asked to “top up” an account or pay for training before you can withdraw earnings. A real employer never asks you to pay to start work.

Online shopping and marketplace scams

Prices far below normal, sellers who push you to pay by bank transfer or leave the platform, or fake delivery and “verification” links. Stay within the platform’s payment system, and be careful with new or unrated sellers.

Fake invoices and changed bank details

An email, apparently from a supplier, solicitor, builder, or boss, says their bank details have changed or asks for an urgent payment. Always confirm any change by phoning a number you already know, not one in the email. This is common when buying a home or paying contractors.

Fake QR codes

Stickers placed over genuine QR codes, such as on parking signs or posters, send you to fake payment pages. Be cautious about scanning codes in public places, check the web address that opens, and prefer the operator’s official app.

AI voice and video fakes

Criminals can copy a voice or face from social media clips. If a “relative” or “colleague” urgently asks for money by call or video, hang up and contact them on a number you already have. A family safe word helps.

How to protect yourself

  • Use strong, different passwords for each account. A password manager makes this easy. Three random words is a good approach for ones you must remember.
  • Turn on two-step verification (2FA) for email, banking, and social media. Where offered, use passkeys.
  • Never share codes sent to your phone, even with someone who sounds official.
  • Keep your phone and apps updated so security fixes are installed.
  • Limit what you share online. Birthdays, addresses, and family details help scammers sound convincing. Review your social media privacy settings.
  • Pay by credit card for bigger purchases. Section 75 can give extra protection on card purchases between £100 and £30,000, so check your card provider’s terms.
  • Set up banking alerts and payment limits in your bank’s app.
  • Check your credit report regularly for accounts you don’t recognise. If your identity has been misused, consider Cifas Protective Registration.
  • Register with the Telephone Preference Service (tpsonline.org.uk) to cut unwanted sales calls. It won’t stop criminals, but it makes unexpected calls more suspicious.
  • Agree a family safe word and talk about scams openly with older relatives and friends.

Things that are never legitimate

  • A bank or the police asking you to move money to a “safe account”.
  • Anyone asking you to buy gift cards or send crypto to settle a debt or fine.
  • Someone who contacted you asking to access your device remotely.
  • A text from HMRC offering a rebate or asking for payment details.
  • A genuine organisation asking for your full PIN, password, or one-time passcode.

Helping someone else

Scams can happen to anyone, however careful. Stay kind and avoid blame. Shame is what keeps people quiet, and criminals rely on that. If someone you know is being targeted, stay calm, help them stop contact, and help them contact their bank and report it.

If it has already happened

  • Contact your bank straight away. Dial 159 to be connected to your bank.
  • Report it to the police via Report Fraud: reportfraud.police.uk or 0300 123 2040 (England, Wales and Northern Ireland). In Scotland, call Police Scotland on 101.
  • Forward scam texts to 7726 (free) and scam emails to report@phishing.gov.uk.
  • Suspicious HMRC texts: forward to 60599 (network charges apply).
  • Change passwords on affected accounts and turn on 2FA.
  • If you’re in immediate danger, call 999.
